# Getting started in day trading — educational material

Course-authored paraphrases and examples, checked against the sources below on 2026-10-07. This is introductory education, not a recommendation to trade. Instructor review is pending. Sources support concepts; this module deliberately avoids regulatory thresholds and claims about current prices or broker-specific rules.

## Learning objectives and beginner priorities

The learner should be able to explain the difference between day trading and longer-term investing, distinguish an order from its execution, recognize trading costs and execution uncertainty, and explain why a planned stop is not a guaranteed loss ceiling. A learner's own answer can provide limited evidence of understanding; hearing an explanation is not evidence of mastery or readiness to trade.

Important beginner ideas to revisit when relevant:

- Day trading is not dependable shortcut income. Substantial losses can occur quickly.
- Borrowing magnifies risk and can leave someone owing more than their initial funds.
- An order does not guarantee execution at the desired price; a price constraint can prevent execution.
- Fees, spreads, liquidity, and slippage matter even when commissions are zero.
- A stop-loss does not guarantee a maximum realized loss.
- Simulated practice does not reproduce every condition of trading with real money.

The four teaching units below are available material, not a compulsory sequence or completion checklist.

## 1. Day trading versus longer-term investing

A **security** is a financial instrument, such as a stock. **Day trading** here means buying and selling the same security within a trading day, seeking gains from short-term price movements. **Longer-term investing** means holding investments over a longer period in pursuit of financial goals, rather than concentrating on intraday movements. Neither approach guarantees profit. FINRA warns that short-term profit seeking is generally less reliable than longer-term investing. [1, 2]

**Risk** is the possibility of an unfavorable outcome, including losing money. Day trading can cause large, immediate losses, including loss of all trading funds. **Margin borrowing** means borrowing money from a brokerage firm to trade. It creates **leverage**: investment exposure greater than one's own committed funds. Borrowing can amplify losses and leave the trader owing more than the original investment. This module does not recommend leverage. [1, 2]

Possible understanding check: How does attempting to profit from today's price changes differ from holding an investment for a longer-term goal?

## 2. Orders and execution

An **order** is an instruction to a broker to buy or sell. **Execution**, also called a **fill**, is completion of that instruction. These are separate events: submitting an order is not proof of the price or even of execution.

- A **market order** seeks execution at the best available price; its final price is not guaranteed.
- A **limit order** permits execution only at a specified price or better; it may remain unfilled.
- A **stop order** becomes a market order when its trigger price is reached; the trigger is not a guaranteed exit price.
- A **stop-limit order** becomes a limit order when triggered; its price constraint may prevent execution.

Order availability and trigger conventions can vary by brokerage firm. These are general explanations, not broker-specific instructions. [3]

Hypothetical example: a sell stop at $50.70 may trigger, but the resulting market order can fill below $50.70. A limit price controls the acceptable price, not whether a willing counterparty will execute the order.

Possible understanding check: Which is not guaranteed by a limit order: its permitted execution price, or that it will execute?

## 3. Costs, spreads, liquidity, and slippage

The **bid** is a quoted buying price and the **ask** is a quoted selling price. The **bid-ask spread** is their difference. For example, a hypothetical bid of $10.00 and ask of $10.05 have a $0.05 spread. **Liquidity** describes how readily an investment can be bought or sold at a fair market price. Low liquidity can cause delays, unfavorable prices, or inability to sell. Wider spreads can reduce returns; a narrow spread alone does not establish sufficient liquidity. [4]

**Slippage** means a difference between an anticipated price and the actual execution price. This is the course's term for execution-price uncertainty described in [3]. Applicable commissions, fees, and borrowing charges also affect outcomes. Frequent trading can accumulate costs; zero commissions do not remove spread or execution uncertainty. [1, 2, 3]

Possible understanding check: Why could repeatedly buying and selling at an unchanged quoted market still cost money?

## 4. Risk, stop-loss limitations, and simulated practice

A **stop-loss** is a planned exit intended to limit loss; it is not a guaranteed execution price. A **gap** is a price jump between trading opportunities. Price changes, low liquidity, halts, or system failures can make an exit differ from the plan. [2, 3]

For this application's hypothetical long stock examples, **maximum planned loss** is the entry-to-stop price difference multiplied by the share count. A **long position** owns shares and benefits from a price increase. Entry $51.20, stop $50.70, and 200 shares give $100.00 before fees, slippage, and a gap through the stop. The calculator measures this supplied plan, not statistical expected loss or a guaranteed ceiling on realized loss. This definition is course-authored application reasoning based on [3], not an SEC definition of the calculator.

**Simulation** means practice without committing real money. Course-authored caution: simulated results depend on assumptions about fills, delays, liquidity, and costs, and do not reproduce every operational or emotional condition of real trading. Success in simulation does not establish that the same outcome will occur with real money. This simulation caution is not attributed to the four sources below.

Possible understanding check: If the calculator reports $100.00, what could cause the actual loss to exceed that figure?
